AI Development Plan

How I designed an AI scenario that turns skill data or an employee’s own request into an individual development plan based on the 70-20-10 model.
Problem
Employees had to identify their own growth areas, turn them into goals, and split actions by the 70-20-10 model. The process was cognitively heavy and took a lot of time.
Task
We needed to add goal generation to an existing IDP service without changing how development plans were stored or edited.
My role
I shaped the product hypothesis, designed the user flow, neural pipeline, system prompts, and interface, and led the project through launch.
Result
The feature launched as a pilot. More than 1,000 employees opened the AI scenario, and over 200 added generated goals to their development plans.

The hard part of IDP was not the form, but the thinking behind it

The individual development plan already existed in the organization as a corporate service. Employees could create goals, add subtasks, track progress, and connect development with specific skills. The problem was not the number of fields or interface complexity. To create a meaningful plan, an employee had to understand what they lacked, choose a priority, turn it into a goal, come up with relevant actions, and distribute them according to the 70-20-10 logic. I framed this as a cognitive load problem and connected it with coverage: the more independent thinking the plan requires, the more likely employees are to fill it in formally or avoid creating it at all.
This is a hybrid case: product work, UX scenario design, and AI solution design. AI was used here to lower the threshold for creating an IDP.

First decision: keep the existing IDP intact

I decided not to build a new product from scratch. A simpler and more reliable path was to generate a draft for the existing IDP. The neural network takes on the most difficult part: it formulates goals, writes descriptions, and suggests tasks. Once the employee saves the result, the draft becomes a regular development plan that can be edited manually. This approach reduced development risk and cost: the bank’s back end continued to work as before, while AI handled only the first and hardest step.

Second decision: split generation into steps

At first I wanted to send one request to the model, but that process was hard to control. I split the task between two AI agents. The first acts as a career assistant: it analyzes skills and suggests growth focuses. A glue layer then combines these focuses with the user’s settings. After that, the second agent assembles the final plan using the 70-20-10 model. To make the generated text land in the right fields automatically, I defined a strict response format for the second agent: title, description, group_70, group_20, and group_10.

Settings became a visual prompt

I designed two scenarios: generation based on SkillsTrack data, the internal skill assessment system, and a free-form scenario where the employee defines the direction. The plan can then be tuned with preferences, difficulty, learning formats, and a focus on hard or soft skills. The same multi-step pipeline works here; only part of the cascade changes. I avoided one large text field so the model would not receive unnecessary context. Instead, I used clear controls in the interface. They show users what can be configured and pass precise parameters to the agent.
After launch, the free-form branch was noticeably more popular. The working materials show an approximate ratio of about 180 added plans without SkillsTrack versus about 20 with SkillsTrack.

Generation quality was controlled manually

During the pilot, generation was not fully stable yet: the model could suggest weak wording, make mistakes, or return a result that needed adaptation to the real work context. I controlled quality manually: tested prompts across different scenarios, reviewed generation examples, and gradually refined the input format and response structure. The interface included loading and error states, plus a warning that the plan should be checked before use.

Results and insights

Development took two sprints. Designing and testing the AI logic started earlier and required more time. By July 2025, the AI plan was already working. The main insight: the free-form scenario was more useful than automated skill analysis. People needed a structure for their own thoughts more than a formal way to close a skill gap.

IDP coverage increased

The funnel: more than 1,000 users opened the scenario, about 40% tried to generate goals, and around 20% of them added the result to their plan. Development plan coverage grew to 42.82% in 2026, compared with 31.34% the year before. The AI plan supported this growth by lowering the entry threshold and giving employees a ready structure. Other projects also affected the metric: the IDP redesign itself and meetups. The AI plan supported coverage growth and attracted an audience, but its exact contribution to the total number still needs to be measured.

Talks and awards

I presented the AI Development Plan at several internal conferences, showing how the product scenario, generative pipeline, and system prompts came together in one service. In 2026, the project won first place at the IT HR Awards in the Technology Company for Employees category.
